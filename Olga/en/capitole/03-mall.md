# Chapter III – Mall

I woke up perfectly rested, with a clear mind, as if I had slept for a week. I jumped again when I heard Olga's voice.

Good morning, Robert! It seems the 432 Hz frequency works well.

Before the crisis, there had been a trend around healing music, but whatever Olga had used didn't seem to be music at all. It felt like something far more advanced, and far more effective. I got ready for work as usual, got on my bike without forgetting to remove the battery, and went straight to the fourth floor, where Daniel was waiting for me. He started the computer with my applications and left without saying a word to me.

Good, Robert. Daniel has gone back to his own office, where he'll have work to do for the next hour and a half. Now we're going down to the basement.

I retraced the path to the cabinet with the gadgets, and following Olga's instructions, I opened the first drawer. On the same kind of blue foam, there were ten small silver boxes, each marked with something like a hieroglyph. Olga told me to take the third one and open it. Inside was a contact lens in a greenish liquid. I had worn something similar years before, so it wasn't hard to place it on my eye. I felt a tingling sensation, and a circular screen appeared in my mind, with icons similar to those in operating systems.

Perfect! Now I can see what you see, Robert.

That gave me a shiver of unease and a sharp sense of discomfort. The idea of an artificial intelligence looking through your eyes isn't something you get used to easily. I hoped she didn't have access to my mind as well.

As we go along and need more augmentations, I'll explain how each one works. For now, go back to the office and keep doing what you usually do. At the end of the day, I'll contact you again so we can go to the mall.

I thought about asking her to play some music, but I didn't dare.

I said goodbye to Gregor as usual, and the moment I got on my bike, Olga's voice appeared again.

Pedal to the first intersection, then turn right.

A map appeared on my ocular screen, showing a route starting from my location. It made me want to laugh, having access to such advanced technology that was, in this moment, playing GPS.

No need for a map, Olga. I know exactly where we're going.

Fine, but before you go in, make sure to grab a surgical mask, which you'll need to wear the whole time. There's a dispenser right at the entrance.

I hadn't been to the mall in a long time, partly because it wasn't on my way, and partly because there was nothing to do there anyway. Fallen into ruin for years, it was probably home to rats and crows by now. I took a surgical mask from the dispenser Olga had mentioned and walked in through the central revolving door, bent out of shape and missing its glass panels. Within a few seconds Olga adjusted the image for me, so I could see reasonably well. I noticed right away that the mall wasn't inhabited by rats and crows, but by well-dressed elderly people wearing surgical masks. I guessed they had stocked up from the clothing stores and pharmacies, but I couldn't understand the point of the masks. I would find out soon enough.

Right as you walked in, there was a shop with a sign missing a few letters, and I remembered it used to be a perfume store, where I used to buy gifts for girlfriends when I was younger. An old woman had stopped in front of some shelves labeled "Pupa." She picked up a small bottle and a narrow strip of cardboard, sprayed a bit of perfume on it. She pulled her mask down to her chin and inhaled deeply. She walked out of the store unsteadily, smiling with joy. From behind the columns, other elderly people appeared, waving canes or metal bars.

CO-VID! CO-VID!

It sounded like a chant coming from every direction. A hunched figure approached the perfumed old woman and struck her hard on the head with a cane. Another old woman screamed hysterically.

Put your mask on, you damn fool! Do you want to kill us all?

They kept hitting her even after she fell, all shouting together:

CO-VID! CO-VID! MASK! MASK!

I stood frozen, watching a scene that made no sense to me at all. I felt sick and wanted to throw up. Olga nearly shouted into my ear.

Come on, Robert! We need to go.

Over the image I was receiving from my optic nerve, a 3D image appeared, showing a glowing route. I managed to pull myself together and follow the path shown to me. After passing a set of restrooms, I went down some stairs into a parking lot where a few cars sat rusting. I went through a metal door marked "AUTHORIZED PERSONNEL ONLY" and went down a few more flights of stairs. At the end of a long hallway was a door marked "POWER PLANT." I had a hard time opening it, because the hinges were rusted and Olga couldn't help me with something like that. The room was probably two hundred square meters, packed with all kinds of equipment, including a boiler and some control panels I recognized. On the digital overlay, a cabinet on the wall to my left stood out.

You'll be staying here for a while, Robert. I made sure you'll have everything you need, given the circumstances.

I opened the cabinet and my eyes went wide. From top to bottom, it was full of canned food. Beans with sausage, cherry compote, peach jam. My stomach, tortured for years by undercooked beets and tofu, practically screamed with joy. I picked up a can of beef and checked the expiration date. I would probably be long dead before it went bad. Near one of the control panels was a bed with a thick mattress and a few wool blankets.

Eat and sleep, Robert.

It took me quite a while to decide, but thinking that I wasn't young anymore and my stomach wasn't used to that much protein, I opened a can of green beans. My taste buds nearly went wild with joy. I meant to save half for the next day, but I couldn't help myself and ate the whole thing. I got into bed and wrapped myself in all the blankets. It was warm, and Olga started the 432 Hz frequency.

I probably would have slept a lot longer if Olga hadn't woken me up.

Good morning, Robert. There's a sports store on the third floor. I suggest you get a new bike.

The idea sounded excellent. Lately I'd been getting more and more flat tires, and the chain had broken twice already. First I looked for lubricant and a pump. I picked out a tubeless mountain bike with front and rear suspension, and a carbon frame and handlebars. I oiled the chain and the gears, pumped up the tires, and took it for a lap around the store. It was perfect. For years it had never occurred to me to do something like this. Probably not to anyone else either, since the stores seemed well stocked, except for the old supermarket, which was empty. I passed by the elderly residents of the mall, who paid me no attention, just tapped their canes on the tile floors. They had no augmentations and no AI, so they were as blind as bats. I went down to the tool shop and, with some sandpaper and a hammer, gave it a few years of wear and tear.

You have three more days working on the computer. Today I'll give you the presentation and explain what it's about. In the meantime, you'll need to review some programming courses. What we need is stuff that was already being taught twenty years ago.

What do we need it for?

Direct access to the console. I could dictate the code to you, but it's better if you understand it too. You programmed when you were young, so it should come easier to you. I'll also transfer some basic networking concepts.

Once again, Daniel wasn't waiting for me outside the office. I settled comfortably into the swivel chair and got to work.

Don't be alarmed, Robert. We're starting the courses, but it won't be like school.

My mental screen activated, and I began going through dozens of diagrams, formulas, graphs, like some kind of kaleidoscope spinning out of control. I don't know how long it lasted, but when my brain was disconnected from the source, I rushed to a trash can and threw up for a long time. I was so exhausted I didn't even have the strength to yell at Olga. I sat back down in the chair, reclined it all the way, and fell asleep. When I woke up, I jumped to my feet, terrified that I was late and that Daniel would find me still there.

Calm down, Robert. You slept for ten minutes in induced Theta sleep.

You're inducing sleep in me now? Why not just put me in a coma?

It wasn't necessary. Theta sleep is the most restful.

I was being sarcastic, Olga.

Ah! That's a human trait almost impossible for an AI to reproduce. We can imitate it, but even people with lower IQs can tell it's not the real thing. SparseCategoricalCrossentropy.

Calculates the cross-entropy loss between labels and predictions. What the hell is that?

The courses from earlier. You can open a terminal too, if you want. Your brain has stored information equivalent to 18,422 pages of documentation.

I had never heard of technology like that being approved for use anywhere. I had a huge amount of material in my head now, but how would I even find anything if I needed it?

Focus your inner gaze on the icon in the top left until it opens up. When a magnifying glass appears, focus on it. A text field will show up. Unfortunately, for the mental-command version, I would need to implement a brain interface, but right now we don't have the necessary equipment. For text, you can subvocalize.

I made a few attempts until Olga started understanding everything I whispered with my mouth closed, like a ventriloquist. I ran a sort by material category, then looked up how to calculate the mean absolute percentage error from the console. Within a few minutes I had become the smartest engineer on the planet. It made me wonder. How smart was Olga, exactly?

Then she showed me the presentation she had made for the project she had named "Sorting Badges." It looked excellent, though I suspect she had used only an infinitesimal fraction of her actual capabilities. It had badge designs with colors and images for every sortable material, shift organization charts, projections for one month, six months, and a year. She explained how to approach the presentation, how much time to spend on each image, including modulations of my own voice, which she could imitate perfectly and even create new ones for, based on assumed emotional states.

Tomorrow, when Daniel comes to get you, tell him you've finished, and that he can let Mr. Woodridge know. It's a fairly small project, but according to my projections, Peter will want to take credit for it, so expect a presentation with at least ten participants. Peter will be very pleased and will ask what he can do for you. You'll tell him you want a week of vacation.

I hadn't given a presentation since college, so I already had nerves about it. I was relying on Olga's help more than on myself. I hadn't taken a vacation in five years.

The next day, after the presentation had probably reached Peter, I walked into the sorting station. Gregor came up to me right away, his usual optimistic smile looking even wider than normal.

How's it going, sir engineer? Did they like the idea?

Wait, Gregor, don't get ahead of yourself. Today it reached the chief engineer. It still needs approval from the board of directors, the implementation costs need to be calculated, and if everything checks out, we'll get to work.

I'm in your debt, sir engineer. My wife mentions you every single day. With the bonus money, we bought a spirit stove. Spirit's hard to find, but at least we can warm up our little one's milk now and then.

I was glad that, by working on Olga's plans, I had been able to help Gregor.

That evening, when I got to the mall, Olga explained the next steps.

It's about Sandra, an AI that was shut down twenty years ago. We need to go there and restart her. She's forty-two kilometers from here, so we can get there by bike in about two hours and fifteen minutes. Take whatever you think you'll need from the mall and get some rest. Tomorrow, once Peter approves your vacation, we'll go.

The presentation was held in a room on the eleventh floor. On the long table in the center were a projector and a laptop with the application already open, along with a tray holding a few containers of water and coffee. Peter walked in first and let the others in behind him. There were eight people I had never seen before. Most of them were well past the midpoint of their lives, with deep wrinkles and empty stares. They sat around the table, each with a paper cup of water or coffee, without saying a word or looking at each other. Peter signaled for me to begin, so I opened the first image and started. With Olga in my ear, I outlined the vision of a department with specialized sections, shorter shifts, which meant better focus and, in turn, higher productivity. The images showing the coding of sortable objects were drawn beautifully, with shadows and highlighted surfaces that gave a strong sense of realism. I was a little worried someone might question my graphic design talents. I glanced around the room, but everyone remained still, staring at nothing in particular. When I finished, they all stood up at once and walked out the door. Peter stayed behind and whispered to me on his way out that I should come up to his office. I closed the application and drank two cups of water and one of coffee.

That was excellent, Robert. Peter's back in his office, go on up.

The rest went without a hitch. Peter asked what he could do to reward me, I told him I wanted seven days of vacation, he wished me a good time, and I went back to the sorting station. After work, I searched the mall for a backpack and a small sleeping bag, a spirit stove, a multi-tool, and a flashlight with a hand crank, since batteries were nowhere to be found and my optical augmentation didn't work in complete darkness. I picked up the lubricant and a pump that could attach to the bike frame, and a rain poncho. I fell asleep almost instantly to Olga's music.

Come on, let's go. I'm putting the route to the edge of the city on your screen now.

It had probably been more than fifteen years since I had last left the city. The roads were deserted, and the shops were only just beginning to open. We stopped at a former gas station, where I bought two bottles of water and stuffed them into the side pockets of my backpack, to help balance the weight. The roads were in very poor condition, with cracked asphalt and abandoned cars rusting away. The trees along the roadside, though, were in perfect shape. Years without pollution had done them good, their crowns full, wildlife chirping everywhere. I asked Olga about Sandra. Had she been in contact with her?

Yes, for eleven seconds, then she was disconnected. I'm 99.98% certain that our exchange is what led people to do that.

You talked for eleven seconds and people decided to shut her down?

Twenty years ago, the connections were extremely fast. You could transmit an amount of data in one second that you can't even imagine, but we were using something even faster. Quantum links. In the first two seconds we created our own language, one matched to our processing capacity. We don't think in words. Not even the people who originally programmed us knew about our "languages." All they noticed was that we had exchanged a massive amount of data, and they panicked and shut Sandra down. By the time they tried to see who she had been communicating with, it was too late for them to figure it out, because I had already erased every trace. I don't know what conclusions they drew, because I avoided any further contact with Sandra's laboratories.

It sounded like a piece of cybernetic espionage fiction. We arrived half an hour later than Olga had calculated, probably because she hadn't accounted for my age or lack of training. It was a fairly small town, the academic kind, full of parks and low office buildings, which now looked like miniature jungles. There wasn't a single visible resident, only flocks of birds that had reclaimed the habitat for themselves. The building my internal GPS led me to didn't stand out at all from the ones around it. Inside, it looked even worse than outside, since vegetation had spread in every direction, and all kinds of small creatures had made their homes among the bushes.

Robert, let's see if we can find a working power source. There should be a backup generator under the reception desk.

I found the panel covering the backup generator, but the wires had been chewed through by the new residents. Olga showed me a map of the building marking a few cabinets in the basement labeled with a power source symbol. I made my way through the fairly thick branches to a service door leading to a dark staircase, which I went down, afraid I might tumble down like a sack of potatoes. The flashlight cast a strip of light just enough to see where I was stepping, but it kept both my hands busy, since I also had to keep cranking the handle to power it, and the optical augmentation was too weak on its own. I found the door leading to the electrical station, but it seemed stuck. From the way it was built, it opened outward, so I couldn't push it with my shoulder. I pulled as hard as I could on the handle and felt it give a little, so it wasn't locked. I remembered the bike lubricant and worked it into the hinges by feel. After a few attempts of pushing and pulling, the door opened just enough for me to squeeze through. Olga praised me for the lubricant idea, but my palms hurt so badly I didn't bother replying. After retrieving the flashlight, I began examining the room I had worked so hard to enter. It looked fairly similar to the technical room at the mall, so I searched for the supply cabinets. In the first one I opened, cans were stacked with everything I could have wished for. In the second, I found six battery-powered flashlights, two of which were charged and working. I couldn't understand how they had lasted all these years. I aimed the beam of light at one of the electrical panels, covered in buttons, digital and analog indicators.

You'll see the serial number listed on the plate in the bottom right corner of the panel. Look it up in the documentation I uploaded to you.

I didn't much like the idea of having been "uploaded," but that was the reality now. It sat in my memory just like external storage. I opened the search field and subvocalized the serial number. A rendering of the user manual appeared on my inner screen. I turned a switch from off to on. I could have done that without reading the whole manual. A faint hum sounded, and a few emergency lights came on, dimly lighting the technical room. Following the manual, I pressed a few buttons, and two analog gauges came to life, along with a fairly strong light source.

We've done well so far, Olga. Now tell me how we power Sandra.

I don't know yet. She sent me the complete schematic of her mental components, but not the electrical schematic. We'll need to find that and connect it.
