# Chapter V – Belgrade

# Chapter IV - Belgrade

The train stopped with that familiar screech of metal: vacations, conferences, parents... Thick steam wrapped around Peter and me, like in a Russian novel full of steppes and beautiful but sad women. The heavy access door opened, surprisingly quiet, and the conductor's face appeared — or rather, his enormous mustache.
- Good health to you, gentlemen! He greeted us with the enthusiasm of someone who rarely gets the chance to talk to anyone. Come aboard.
I grabbed one of the support bars, then reached out my hand to Peter and pulled him into the cabin fairly easily. It seemed all that cycling had made me stronger. Peter looked at me, surprised, then shook the conductor's hand.
- Peter Woodridge, he introduced himself, then pointed at me. Robert Martinovici, my assistant.
I shook the man's hand too, wondering whether to correct Peter — but I told myself assistants could be engineers as well.
- Aram Hachaturian, the conductor introduced himself, and suddenly his mustache made a lot more sense.
I had never been inside a locomotive before, and I felt the excitement of a kid tasting one of life's small wonders. Maybe only a submarine could have thrilled me more. Aram showed us to our seats, two roomy berths on either side of the corridor.
- Make yourselves comfortable. I'll start up the beast, and then we can talk.
Aram began pulling all kinds of levers, and the train started moving with a terrifying noise. Once the train settled into cruising speed, Aram appeared again, pushing a wheeled cart carrying a huge samovar and several steel cups. What truly amazed me was a bowl full of sugar cubes sitting on the shelf under the cart. My mouth started watering, and out of the corner of my eye I noticed Peter wiping a bit of drool from the corner of his mouth. I thought to myself that even the higher-ups didn't have everything.
Aram filled our cups with tea and glanced toward the sugar.
- Help yourselves, gentlemen. Around Belgrade we have sugar beet fields as far as the eye can see.
He poured some tea from his cup into a small saucer, put a sugar cube between his teeth, and melted it with tea from the saucer. I remembered a classmate telling us back in college that this was how Russians drank their tea.
- Are you from Serbia or from Russia, Mr. Aram?
- Aram is enough, and yes, I live in Serbia, but I was born in Yerevan, in Armenia. You have a sharp eye, sir engineer. Yes, we drink tea this way too, like the Russians. Or maybe the Russians drink it like us? Who knows.
- I've never been farther than Baikal, Peter admitted. Did you leave Armenia long ago?
I nearly jumped. Peter had been to Siberia?
- A long time ago, Peter Peterovich. I was a child when my parents decided it was better to be closer to the West, and back then Serbia was as West as it got. Wait a moment, sir engineer! Baikal? That's the middle of nowhere!
- Yes, Aram. That was back when relations were very close, and the delegations lasted so long they often ended in marriages.
So Aram had noticed too. Had it been a slip? Was it meant for me? For Olga? But Peter, in theory, didn't know about Olga. I decided to pretend I hadn't noticed anything. I turned my attention back to Aram.
I looked at his laugh lines, his tired eyes, his fairly noticeable bald spot. He seemed a bit older than me, but not by much.
- What did you study, Mr. Aram?
The Armenian burst into a deep laugh, showing a row of very white teeth.
- Sir engineer! Can you read the stars? Yes, I graduated from the Faculty of Railway Engineering at the Belgrade Polytechnic. In other words, a train conductor with a university degree.
- I'm no fortune teller, just observant.
Peter looked at me from under his eyebrows, as if seeing me for the first time after decades of not paying attention.
- So you're always traveling, Mr. Aram? Peter asked.
- I travel a lot, but I have two months a year when I stay home. And when I say home, I mean truly home. On the porch, pipe in my mouth, with a stack of old newspapers. I know more about what's happening in the world than the people back home do, but old habits are worth keeping. I read, and I compare it to real life. Speaking of real life, we're approaching the checkpoint.
- Checkpoint? What checkpoint? Did we leave Schengen? Peter asked, alarmed.
- Stay calm, sir engineer. It's internal. A barter zone. Everything's arranged by your colleague. I even have the manifest, if you want to know exactly what we're paying.
- Honestly, I am curious, Peter said. I don't need to know who approves it, just what the barter is made of here.
Aram dug through the pockets of his jacket and pulled out a crumpled sheet of paper.
- Here you go: 50 pairs of cotton socks, 20 blue polyester shirts, 100 tampons...
- That's enough, Mr. Aram. I get the idea, I said.
- No need to worry. This is no man's land here. Further along there's a stopping point. That's where we hand over the goods. It goes fast — nobody counts anything, and they have a few quick young men working there. Besides, we come back through here anyway. Come with me, it'll be interesting.
We got off right next to the stopping point, a building with two rooms and a wood stove burning strongly. Two sturdy men were waiting for us at the door.
- Come in, said one of them, with a strange accent, part Slavic and part German. Last time Aram brought us some brandy from home, and we thought we'd share it with our friends while the young ones unload the goods.
- Great idea, Peter said. We brought some Prague ham too.
I stared at Peter, shocked, as if seeing him for the first time. In a way, that's exactly what was happening. I had never seen him take control of a situation like an experienced smuggler. And Prague ham was a product I hadn't heard mentioned in a very long time.
