# Chapter I – Cold

My head hurt and my sinuses were blocked. It was still dark, but the night lamp gave off enough light to see where I was stepping. I wouldn't even have needed it, because in a fifteen-square-meter studio with a bed, a table, and a wardrobe, you can manage easily enough. I sat on the edge of the bed, spine straight, chin up, tongue pressed against the roof of my mouth. I began the adjustment breathing: four seconds in, four seconds holding, four seconds out. After a minute I started meditating. In the classes, they had shown us tropical landscapes, with palm trees, an ocean stretching endlessly, and an orange sun. Unfortunately, they hadn't shown us how warm it actually was, because thermal restrictions had been in place for more than twenty years. They told you to rub your palms together hard, and when you felt the warmth, to press them against your eyes and think about the orange sun. It never worked for me. I was always cold.

I took my soap and toothbrush from the wardrobe and went out into the hallway. Only the neighbor from 16BD was at the bathroom, so it wasn't five o'clock yet. We greeted each other with a glance, and I took my turn at the personal hygiene stall. That's what the building manager called it, and that's what I called it too. People need a common language, don't they? The shower didn't work, but I didn't want to wash with cold water either. I pulled my hat off my head, stuffed it in my pocket, and splashed some water on my face, just enough to get my body used to it a little. I took a mouthful of very cold water and rinsed my mouth, skipping the toothbrush, then washed my hands for twenty seconds. No law had ever been passed about brushing your teeth. I put my hat back on and went back to my room. It seemed a little warmer already. I changed into my work clothes and went downstairs, out in front of the building. It had started to get light, and the clear sky showed signs of good weather ahead. Every day, the officials explained that the climate protection laws were working perfectly and that within ten years at most, we would enjoy warmth again. To me it was a promise with no real meaning, since it was unlikely I would still be alive by then. I wanted to check my watch, but I remembered the batteries had died the month before, and given how expensive they were, I would only replace them with my next paycheck. I got on my bicycle and rode toward work, along with hundreds of other employees. The sorting station wasn't far from my building, and in about fifteen minutes I was locking my bike in the parking area. With my ID badge fluttering in my left hand, I slipped through the turnstiles and headed toward my workstation. The huge hall looked almost empty, only a few small figures carrying wicker baskets, known as "bio-baskets."

Good morning, sir engineer.

Gregor seemed happy with life, as always. Born in the Donbas region of Ukraine, he seemed to have an inhuman resistance to the cold. Even though it was probably no more than ten degrees, Gregor was wearing nothing but a shirt.

Good morning, Gregor. How did the night shift go? Did we meet the targets?

Gregor looked at the flip chart, underlined something with a dull pencil, and turned back to me.

Not really, sir engineer. We're doing fine with glass, fine with plastic, but the diapers and the tampons are killing us. I don't understand why they haven't passed a law forcing women to switch to biodegradable textile material. People try to avoid sorting them, and I can't put together special teams just for that.

Make one-hour rotations, Gregor.

The Ukrainian looked at me the way you'd look at a good friend whose mind wasn't quite up to the job.

You know we don't have enough people for quality control. They'll probably just grab all the glass instead. It's not like I can put badges on people... Wait a moment, that's actually not a bad idea. Could you talk to management about making badges that say glass, plastic, diapers?

I thought about whether I should tell him about the yellow stars Jewish people had worn during the Nazi period, but I decided the idea was good, so I planned to present it to the people in charge of Standards.

Gregor, I think you just earned yourself a bonus. Keep things running here, please — I'm going to make the most of this while it's still fresh.

If I had had a laptop like in my younger years, I probably would have made a nice presentation, with some predictions based on the last two years of data, but since the crisis had caused energy consumption to drop by almost eighty percent, anything that ran on electricity and wasn't absolutely necessary had been removed from everyday life. I picked up a flip chart and a pencil from Gregor's table and started sketching a few graphs, relying on intuition and experience. I also made a fairly crude table classifying recyclable materials, and went to schedule a meeting.

The management building was quite far from the collection center, so I picked up my pace. The sun was fairly high in the sky, and it had warmed up enough that I took off my hat and stuffed it into my pants pocket, as usual. The trees planted over the last ten years looked healthy, their leaves green, some already budding. The grass was in worse shape, since it wasn't watered enough and had wilted a little. No flowers were planted, since they offered no real climate benefit. I opened the heavy door at the entrance of the management offices and stopped in front of the reception desk. Gina, a young, smiling woman, looked at me closely, as if I had no reason to be there.

Mr. Martinovici, how can we help you today?

I need a meeting with the chief engineer.

Regarding what?

The question struck me as odd, coming from a receptionist. Maybe a new visitor screening system had been introduced, except I wasn't a visitor. I was working.

Regarding improving performance.

Ah! Let me check. Please wait a few minutes.

Gina was obviously using a laptop. You can't schedule a meeting on a flip chart to bring to the chief engineer. She typed away at the keyboard for a while, then told me, sounding a bit surprised,

The chief engineer can see you now.

I took the elevator up to the eleventh floor. What a great invention, elevators. As long as you have electricity. I tucked my shirt in a little better, ran my fingers through my hair, and knocked on the door frame, since the door itself was padded, so knocking on it wouldn't have made sense. I heard a muffled "come in" and pushed the door open with my shoulder, knowing how heavy it was. The office was about the size of my studio apartment, but bright and well-furnished. The chief engineer was standing with a cup of coffee in his hand. The smell of coffee always brought back memories from before the crisis.

Come in, Robert. Want some coffee?

Of course I wanted some, so I nodded. He prepared me a cup at the espresso machine and turned back to me, eyebrows raised in question. We had known each other for years, we had been classmates in college, but his family ties to people high up in the ruling party had pushed him toward leadership positions. I didn't envy him, because that's how things worked in the world we lived in, and besides, he was a decent guy. He looked a few years younger than me, which made sense for someone who didn't have to meditate on a tropical sun every morning just to avoid freezing to death. The room was probably warmer than sixteen degrees, and the sun shining through the generous windows made it feel even warmer.

Go ahead, Robert. What can I do for you?

I walked up to the desk with the flip chart in one hand and the pencil in the other. Peter looked a little surprised at my presentation equipment, but he knew I didn't have the rank to be issued a tablet. I started telling him about the problems we were having with the diapers and tampons, without forgetting to mention Gregor's contribution. I showed him the numbers and made a few projections so he could see what kind of benefits we might see over the next six months. I couldn't calculate what would happen a year out without access to a computer, and the government wasn't interested in what would happen a year from now anyway, except when it came to staying in power. I said nothing about Jewish people, since any subject that might lead to the word "holocaust" could carry social consequences that would be hard to control. Peter listened closely and took some notes on his tablet.

Robert, I like this. Here's what we'll do. You get two hours a day of access to a personal computer. I want a real presentation, with graphs, projections, shifts, maintenance, everything. Do you think you can finish it in a week?

A personal computer, two hours a day, for a week?

Yes, Peter, I'll finish it. When do I start?

I'll call IT now to set up your account, and you can start tomorrow.

He opened a desk drawer and pulled out a handful of AAA batteries.

Since you're already here, don't leave empty-handed. Don't look at me like that, I'm not giving you charity. At least get your watch working again until payday.

I took the batteries without feeling insulted in the least. He had them, and I needed them.

On my way out, Gina said goodbye almost warmly.

It was nearly five in the afternoon, so I got Gregor and we headed to the conference room. In a few minutes the government news program would start, and if you didn't want a pay cut, you sat through half an hour watching, or pretending to watch, whatever the officials had to say. An older man with the look of an intellectual was pointing at a colorful map, showing us which global environmental problems remained. He went through them one by one: climate, pollution, oceans, energy, biodiversity, and so on. The map switched from one topic to another, or was replaced by graphs, animated clips, or large warning signs. The next topic was more interesting, though no one was convinced that what was being presented was actually true. On a map covering the entire wall, you could see the countries of the Union. The ones that had left were shown in black, and the ones that wanted to leave were gray. Their names weren't shown, but the older people knew which was which. From Portugal all the way to Taiwan, the world looked like a wheel of Swiss cheese. The announcer's voice was cheerful, and the commentary was full of optimism. Tajikistan had been accepted into the Union, and Ukraine was about to join Schengen. Gregor puffed out his chest with pride and shot me a look that said, "I told you so." At the end, Marc van der Bloom, Secretary General of the Union, appeared and advised us to keep our energy consumption low and enjoy the arrival of spring.

I said goodbye to Gregor and went to get my bicycle. It had already gotten dark, so I took out one of the batteries Peter had given me and put it in my flashlight. I felt envious eyes on me as the beam of light cut through the darkness. I felt like I was overdoing it, using electricity for something so trivial, but I figured I deserved one evening where I could get home without falling into some ditch. Once home, I opened a can of corn, looking at the flip chart I had brought with me. I took out the slide rule, a family heirloom made of cedar wood and Bakelite, and started doing some preliminary calculations. I wanted to make the most of the time I had been given to use the computer. I thought maybe a few more ideas would come to me over the next few days, and I could present them to management. I woke up frozen and numb. I had fallen asleep with my head resting on my arm, which was now tingling horribly. My head hurt, so I took my hat out of my pocket and pulled it down over my ears. I fumbled for the alarm clock and put two batteries in it. I still had one more in my pocket, and one in the bike's flashlight, which I had forgotten to take out. I hoped I would still find it there in the morning. The clock started blinking, showing 12:00. I would have to bring it to work with me to set it against the clock at the station. I looked through the small window of my room, but all I could see were stars, so morning was still a while away. Lying in bed, blanket pulled over my head, I went back over the previous day. In the cold of the room, under the pale light of the night lamp, the difference between how I lived and how Peter lived felt very large, and honestly irritating. Before the crisis, even though Peter had been just as high up on the social ladder, it hadn't bothered me at all. He probably went to fancy parties, but I had my own parties too. After deciding whose place we'd go to, ideally a friend with a house and a yard, a group of about ten of us would gather, buy meat for the grill, drinks, and whatever else we wanted, and spend the whole weekend having fun. If we were cold, we turned on the heating. If we were hot, we turned on the air conditioning. When exactly had everything changed? And more importantly, how? Ever since, I had felt that the people running things had no idea what they were supposed to be doing.

Even though I was very cold, I washed my upper body with the dry soap I had owned for several years. Detergents were pollutants, so they taught you that a cloth dampened with water was enough to maintain a reasonably clean body. I had no intention of walking into IT smelling like a dead animal. The battery was where it belonged, but I made a mental note to take it out as soon as I got to work. Peter hadn't told me what hours I was allowed to use the computer, so I went straight to the management building. Gina welcomed me with a smile a little wider than the last time.

Good morning! Mr. Woodridge let me know what you need to do. Please take the elevator to the fourth floor. Mr. Daniel will guide you.

I thanked her and took the elevator to the floor I assumed was assigned to the IT department. A friendly-looking young man was waiting in front of the elevator, smiling almost as widely as Gina. He shook my hand and pointed the way.

You probably haven't worked with a PC in a while, but not much has changed. The monitors are bigger, and the applications are more intuitive.

Actually, I was nervous about how computers worked these days, after fifteen years of not having touched one. He opened the door to an office and invited me in. It was a large room with about twenty empty desks, each with a huge monitor and a swivel chair. Daniel gestured for me to sit wherever I liked and started the computer from his tablet. On the screen, the thirty-nine stars of the Union appeared, rotated slightly, and settled into the shape of a hexagon.

Mr. Woodridge told me you need a presentation application, and maybe something for calculating and visualizing data.

Daniel opened two applications that resembled what I used to work with, then said goodbye and left. I worked through the two allotted hours at a fast pace, mostly familiarizing myself with the changes. When I finished, I saved my work, and Daniel walked in through the door. I assumed he had some kind of connection to the computer through his tablet that told him when I saved, but then I remembered I had saved twice before that, so there was probably a camera on the monitor, or one somewhere in the office. I planned to check the next day.

As soon as I got to the sorting station, I took my watch out of my pocket and set it against the one on the wall. It was already 10:30, so I went to the cafeteria for breakfast. Gregor was waving excitedly from one of the tables. I signaled him to be patient, and after putting my two tofu sandwiches and a cup of tea on my tray, I went to sit next to him.

Thank you so much, sir engineer! They told me this morning I got a raise for the badge idea.

I was surprised to see Peter had remembered and moved so quickly.

You're welcome, Gregor. You deserve it fully.

The Ukrainian looked at me with gratitude, and it made me think about the conditions he lived in with a wife and two children. I assumed a foreman earned less from the state than an engineer did. We had worked together for five years, but apart from a few general facts — wife, children, hometown — I knew nothing about him. Since bars no longer existed, considered heavy energy consumers, people no longer had much of a social life.

We went back to the station together and worked until five. After the government briefing ended, Gregor shook my hand with more enthusiasm than usual, and we each headed our own way.

I lay in bed, looking at the night lamp, thinking about the two hours I had spent at the computer, with no one watching over my shoulder, even though I was sure I had been watched the whole time. I hadn't seen anything that looked like a camera on the monitor, but then I wasn't up to date on the newer models. In the old days, they used to be specifically designed so you'd notice when you were being filmed, except any half-decent hacker could turn off the warning light and still send footage of whatever fell inside the frame. The office was large enough to hide at least ten cameras. If they had high resolution, they could have seen exactly what was on the monitor in detail, but what would be the point when there were cheaper methods available, like a keylogger. I had some suspicions I needed to check in person. I fell asleep thinking about video cameras turning to search for me. Reality turned out to be far more surprising.
