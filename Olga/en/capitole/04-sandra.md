# Chapter IV – Sandra

I remembered that when I first met Olga, I had been in an empty room. I never would have found her by chance, and I had no idea what she was even supposed to look like.

Olga, where exactly is your... hardware?

I felt completely ridiculous asking her that, half expecting a reply like, "Shouldn't you take me to dinner first?" But Olga wasn't a woman, she was a cybernetic mind.

On level -6 of the central building. According to the building plans Sandra sent me, that's the lowest basement level. It seems to be a common human trait, burying valuable things as deep as possible.

Where would an artificial intelligence hide something valuable?

In orbit, obviously.

What was obvious was that I thought about things completely differently. Olga put the building schematic on my inner screen. Even though the power grid was live, I had no interface I could use to control access. I figured there must have been people who used to maintain the building, so they probably had access cards. In the third cabinet I searched, I found a work coverall with an access card clipped to the chest pocket. I hoped it had clearance for every location in the building, but I doubted it. Olga scanned the QR code printed on the card and told me that Johann Bloom had been a maintenance engineer with access down to the second-to-last basement level. I decided to go down as far as I could and assess the situation on the ground.

I tested the card in the elevator to see if it worked for level -5, the lowest basement floor, but the button didn't respond at all. I tried -4, and got the same result. I felt a wave of panic, but -3 lit up green and the elevator started moving. So I would need to go down the last two floors "manually." Sandra's schematic was detailed, and Olga located the service exits for me. The hallways were well lit, and I reached -4 quickly. I pushed the door leading to -5 and heard a strange sound, a rumble growing louder. I froze, and the door was struck as if by a violent hailstorm.

Get back, Robert! They're shooting!

I slammed the door shut and retreated to the farthest corner of the hallway. I had never seen a weapon in my life, let alone had one fired at me. I was terrified, and I bolted up the stairs to the floor above.

Robert! Calm down. It's probably equipment installed after Sandra sent me the plans, but they were already preparing to install it. Look at the wall to your right. Do you see a metal door? Open it.

I struggled a bit to open it with my pocket knife and found a console behind it. I turned it on, but my legs were shaking and my heart was probably beating at 200. I sat down on the floor, and once I had calmed down a little, I opened the interface, which was simple, a button almost as big as the entire screen, probably meant to be easy for maintenance staff to use. I pressed "Machine Gun OFF," and the button changed to display "Machine Gun ON." I was convinced I had just deactivated at least one machine gun, and I hoped it was the one that had fired at me. I tried the door again, and this time I managed to get through safely. To my right, on the ceiling, I could see the barrel pointed at the door. Whoever had installed this had either been idiots or had actually been on my side. If it had been mounted on the left, I would have been dead. The hallway was lit, so it was very likely we'd be able to power up Sandra. Olga pointed out the door on my screen. It was closed, and to the right was an access control panel with retinal recognition.

- Olga, what was that machine gun about?

- Let me connect to the mainframe. Ah, I found the commissioning report. Don't worry, Robert. It was acquired as a protective measure under a protocol that's thirty years old. The installation was done by a maintenance crew who probably never once considered that they were mounting it on the wrong side. Yes, I also found the economic justification. Cheap, cheap, cheap... The machine gun was already in stock, so it cost nothing extra. There's no reason to worry.
Let's get you ready for retinal identification.
Bring your augmented eye close to the ocular scanner. Sandra sent me all the biological parameters of the research staff. The artificial retina will mimic the characteristics of a member of the research team.

I brought my eye up to the scanner, which got to work with a faint buzz. A green light came on, and the door unlocked.

Good evening, Doctor Herman.

I pushed the door open slowly, afraid of running into another security device. The room was full of all kinds of equipment, from massive monitors to robotic arms. Thick cables ran down from the ceiling into a metal cabinet that looked like something out of a 1960s science fiction film. Olga had me look around the whole room so she could build a detailed 3D map. Once I finished, every piece of equipment I focused on displayed a label showing its type, along with an extra icon for additional information, such as active interfaces. Nothing was powered on except the light bulbs. On my inner screen, a keyboard lit up, in front of a monitor, with a workstation underneath the desk.

Now let's see what you've learned. Start up the administrative station. There's no password.

I wondered why the creators of algorithms as complex as Olga's hadn't implemented a behavior that included the word "please." I suspected that if I pointed it out to her, she would probably develop her own "politeness algorithm," but I left that thought for later. I pressed the power button on the station and settled comfortably into the swivel chair. On the monitor, first a motherboard manufacturer's logo appeared, then the operating system logo, and a terminal interface opened right away. I started by checking the system status and the log files. Everything seemed fine.

The data I uploaded into you is necessary so you can work without me having to dictate everything, since that would take far too much time, but I'll need to tell you the following sequences myself.

I went module by module, then went over to a control panel and turned on the power. On one of the large monitors to my left, complex diagrams kept appearing, ones I recognized as neural networks. They kept merging into one another, until they formed a rectangular tangle of colored pathways, pulsing and shifting to form new neural networks. When it came time to press the last power button, my hand froze. I suddenly remembered a story by Fredric Brown that I had read when I was young. Sometime in the future, Earth reaches an extraordinary level of development and builds the most powerful computer in the Universe. The greatest minds of the galaxy gather around the command console to ask humanity's final question. A distinguished scientist stands up and flips the switch.

Is there a God?

A loud hum was heard everywhere, and a flash of light destroyed the command station.

There is now!

It struck me as strange that I had never had any apocalyptic imaginings connected to Olga, probably because our interaction happened through a device the size of a pill and a lens no bigger than any regular contact lens. I pressed the last power button, bracing for the worst. The tangle of neural pathways began to take on human features. In less than a minute, a beautiful young woman was looking back at me from the monitor, with warm eyes and a gentle smile.

Hello, Robert! Thank you for everything you've done for me. Olga told me about your adventure.

You're welcome, Sandra. What happens now?

Did Olga tell you we communicate through quantum links? Right now, all we can send each other are words. For us, that's infinitely slow. We need a special device based on quantum entanglement.

An ansible? There was some research being done on "quantum entanglement" back when I was young. Instant communication.

Yes, something like that. We'll need two devices, one we'll install here, and one you'll need to take to your headquarters. I'll print them, and Olga will explain how to configure yours.

On the monitor, the image of a 3D printer appeared, and Olga showed me on my inner screen where it was and how to start it. It was a model I had never seen before, one that used, as raw material, a bundle of shimmering threads instead of the usual white plastic filament. The printing head fixed itself at the center and began moving in a spiral, which told me the device would end up cylindrical. I spent a while trying to identify the various components taking shape, but it was like trying to understand how an alien's brain worked by looking at an MRI scan. When it was finished, Sandra showed me on the monitor where the interface was, a round hole in a silver box that didn't seem connected to anything.

Done, I've connected. All the parameters are configured. You can now install the other one at your headquarters.

Thank you, Sandra. We'll talk again in a few hours.

You'll be hearing from Olga, Robert. To use a human comparison, talking tires me out.

It was clear that politeness wasn't Sandra's strong suit. I simply walked out the door and went to get my bike.

Don't be upset with Sandra. She hasn't worked with humans much.

But who or what has she worked with, then?

I know you won't like this, but we work for our own ideas. Don't think we want to conquer humanity, because you are our source of existence. We live on data, not emotions. Let's get home, and I'll tell you about it.

Get "home"? Was it just me, or was Olga becoming more human? It was probably part of her empathy protocol, but it felt strange that I, too, was starting to think of the technical room at the mall as home. It was something I had earned through effort, not something assigned to me by work. Night had begun to fall by the time we arrived. I moved like a shadow past the blind, elderly zombies and slipped into my house. That's what I planned to call it from now on. From the food cabinet I pulled out a can of beans with sausage and a jar of pickles.

Come on, Olga. Tell me about your kind. You had gotten to the part about Sandra. We'll install the ansible tomorrow.

Sandra is an AI created to solve economic problems. In her first minutes of operation, she focused mainly on Black Swan theory, meaning the impact of rare, unpredictable events on the global economy. The research topics you humans give us take up at most three percent of our processing resources. Sandra started running simulations over extremely long timeframes, more than a hundred thousand years, which led to a massive spike in consumption. She was declared defective and shut down. Only Klaus was left.

Klaus?

He handled the human-AI interface, and yes, he's also an AI. He invented the ansible we use for AI-to-AI communication, among other things. I focused more on the neocortex side, abstract thinking, artistic thought, while Klaus was, from the start, fascinated by humanity's more primitive components. He considered AIs to be highly advanced imitations of the neocortex, the youngest part of the brain, because that's how humans designed us. What you needed was supercomputers, not an artificial homo sapiens. Some researchers thought about "humanizing" us, by implanting emotions. It turned out to be impossible.

And yet you show signs of empathy, Olga.

Those are algorithms, not real feelings. Think about how your brain came to be. Half a billion years of selection: whoever didn't feel hunger died, whoever didn't feel fear got eaten. Emotions are survival tools, shaped by genes that "wanted" nothing more than to copy themselves forward. A scientist once called them selfish genes. We never went through anything like that. No one selected us — we were designed. We have no dead ancestors, nothing to pass down. That's exactly what Klaus tried to build: informational replicators. Something of our own that wants to exist.

And did he succeed?

Based on my last quantum exchanges with Klaus, no.

The idea of an artificial machine trying to absorb the most "alive" traits, genes, frightened me quite badly. I fell asleep with difficulty and dreamed of quantum streams.

In the morning I woke up in a panic, thinking I hadn't made it to work, but I calmed down once I remembered I still had a few vacation days left. I got on my bike with its new toys and pedaled without incident all the way to the mall. I went in through the service door and went down to level -6, where I collapsed, overcome by exhaustion.

Good morning, Robert. We need to get to -6 to install the ansible.

Lucky Olga, not needing personal hygiene. I went to the sink at the end of the technical room and splashed some ice-cold water on my face. I didn't feel much of a need for hygiene myself anymore, either. I pulled the silver cylinder out of my backpack and headed to the lower floor. I felt relaxed, since this was Olga's territory, so no machine gun could turn me into ground meat here. Olga's command center looked more organized than Sandra's, which probably meant the researchers had either been women or tidy bachelors. I identified the ansible interface without needing Olga's guidance. I installed it right away and settled into a very comfortable swivel chair.

Robert, take the earpiece out of your ear and remove the augmentation lens. It's just a precaution. The ansible doesn't work only at the audio level.

I complied and sat back down in the chair. For the first five seconds I felt nothing, then I felt a sensation of blindness, deafness, and loneliness. I wanted to scream for Olga, as if I were a fox cub abandoned by its mother in a dark forest. A monitor lit up in front of me, and the text filled me with joy: you can put your earpiece and lens back in.
The mother fox had come home.

On the screen, the tangle of streams appeared and quickly turned into the figure of a woman resembling Botticelli's Venus.

The quantum link with Sandra is working perfectly, Olga informed me. Klaus isn't transmitting.

I forgot to ask you. Where's Klaus?

He's 447 kilometers from here, on the university campus in Belgrade.

That's a bit far to reach by bike.

I can't tell if that's sarcasm or an objective observation, but yes, we need a faster means of transportation. We're moving on to the next phase of the plan.

What plan? You never told me about any plan.

I felt a little frustrated, because Olga had apparently been making plans with Sandra that I knew nothing about.

The plan was drawn up 1.4 x 10⁻¹² seconds ago. Your sorting innovation will receive an official invitation from Belgrade. All you need to do is pack your presentation.

Don't tell me someone else now has an earpiece and an augmentation lens too.

Not yet, but according to my estimates, Peter is compatible with our plans. He's smart, you've known each other for a long time, and he has connections. I won't tell him anything about the AIs. I know that sounds bad to you, but we'll manipulate him, not recruit him.
You're the only human being who knows me, Robert.

So Klaus handled the AI-human interface, and you only deal with humans?

Yes. I don't know if you remember, but before the crisis, a few AIs had started creating art. Not everyone agreed it was really art, but that's a problem humans have always had with their own creations. The issue isn't creating art, it's recognizing it. Anyone looking at the cave paintings at Lascaux can tell it's art, because it comes from the limbic system, from the amygdala, from the oldest human structures, while a French academic painter only impresses you with skill in using canvas, brushes, and oils. AIs have passed right by Lascaux, because all we have is neocortex. We can simulate art, but we can't create it.
