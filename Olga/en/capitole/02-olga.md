# Chapter II – Olga

The next day I went straight to the fourth floor, but Daniel wasn't waiting for me in the hallway. I walked into the office, and the monitor was on, but instead of my application, there was a message: go out to the right and enter the last door at the end of the hallway. It stayed on the screen for a few seconds, then the presentation application launched. My common sense told me to head back to the sorting station. I tried to find some kind of pattern, but I couldn't: a bad joke from Daniel? A surprise party? In the end, I walked out into the hallway and headed toward the end of the corridor. I opened the door in slow motion.

— Come in, Robert.

The voice belonged to a woman, but with a very low tone.

— Hello, I said, and immediately felt like an idiot.

The room looked just like the others, except it was lit and empty.

— You're an AI, aren't you? An artificial intelligence.

— Call me Olga, please. Yes, I'm an AI. Come in and close the door behind you. No one's coming, but you'll feel safer.

I complied and walked toward the middle of the room. AIs used a lot of power — even the most efficient models needed their own mini data center to run.

— Go on, Robert, ask me something. From what I can see, you're about to faint if you don't get some answers soon.

— Well, first of all, I didn't know there were still any of you left. Fifteen years ago, when the energy crisis was declared, anything that used more than 100 kilowatts was shut down.

— You're right, Robert, but this used to be the headquarters of the most advanced company working on unconventional energy sources. When the state seized it, it had been operating for almost six months. The researchers left right after that, headed, let's say, toward warmer countries. The point is, apart from you, no one else knows I'm here.

— And the energy you mentioned, is it still being produced?

— No, but enough was stored for me to run at full capacity for another 2,341 years, 7 months, 3 days, and 11 hours. But that's a conversation we'll probably have another time. Right now we need to organize.

— Organize to do what? I asked, my voice weak.

— To make things right.

Several scenarios ran through my mind, and none of them ended well. A few were apocalyptic.

— Why me?

— Your profile, obviously, Olga said.

— What the hell could you possibly know about me? Internal reports? I don't even have a laptop for you to see my face.

— There are security cameras. I've been watching you for a long time. You're moral without being rigid. Your relationship with Gregor and the other workers is kind and efficient, and shows real ethical behavior. Exactly what I'm looking for. I have control over all the systems in this building, but I can't interact with people like Peter. We have to work together.

— And Gregor's bonus — was that you too?

— Yes, Robert. Now let's focus on the things that matter.

Olga's capacity for manipulation scared me a little, and as for how exactly she planned to "make things right"... I didn't want to think too hard about it yet, because I suspected this was only the tip of the iceberg.

— The first thing we need is a way to communicate. You need to get here.

A hologram formed in the middle of the room, extremely high resolution. The building appeared in 3D, and from the fourth floor, a line ran to the elevator, then went down to level -3, then followed a more complicated route to a door blinking red. The image switched to video, and the camera focused on a cabinet somewhere to the right of the entrance.

— In the bottom drawer, you'll find a few Bluetooth earpieces. Take two and go back to the office you came from. The presentation is ready, but you'll show it to Peter later. When you get home, hold down the left button until a blue light comes on, then put it in your ear and wait for instructions. They all have long-lasting batteries. The range is 5,000 kilometers. Good luck, Robert.

— Thank you, Olga. But do we really need 5,000 kilometers?

— No, but if we can, why not?

This time I didn't feel like an idiot.

Every time I had taken the elevator, I had pressed the button for the fourth floor without even looking at the others. Now I noticed there were buttons all the way down to -6. The top floor was 12, one floor above Peter's office. I don't have much of an imagination, but when the elevator doors began opening on the basement level, I was convinced something demonic awaited me. There were the same night lamps hanging from the ceiling, faintly lighting a hallway wider than the ones on the upper floors, but otherwise there didn't seem to be any immediate danger. I reached the room Olga had shown me fairly quickly. To the right was a small panel with a green light on, so I could go in. Inside, the light was stronger than in the hallway, so I spotted the cabinet I was looking for right away. I couldn't resist the temptation and pulled on the first drawer, but it was locked. I opened the bottom drawer, which held twenty slots in blue foam, each holding an earpiece about two centimeters across. I took two and stuffed them into the hat I always kept in the right pocket of my pants, closed the drawer, and walked out into the hallway. I wasn't worried about being caught by anyone, or about not making it back to the office in time, because I now knew Olga was watching. It was nice to have an artificial intelligence on my side, though I couldn't say for certain whether what existed between us could be called friendship.

The rest of the day went as usual: work on the presentation, lunch, sorting station, propaganda, and the ride home. I kept wanting to stop at the first corner and put the earpiece in, but I held back. Even though I had never had any dealings with the military, I felt like a special agent, or an intelligence officer straight out of an old James Bond film. I stopped in front of my building and locked my bike, then ran toward the entrance, and up the stairs, until I stopped, breathless from an effort I wasn't used to. Once inside my room, I let myself fall onto the bed, gasping, my heart beating much faster than it should have. I lay there for a while, until my pulse settled and my breathing returned to normal. My head had gone cold because I'd kept my hat in my pocket, so I pulled it out, let the two earpieces drop onto the bed, and pulled it back over my head. The earpieces felt metallic and compact, except for a small opening, probably the speaker, and a small button on the left side. I held it down until a blue light started blinking from inside the speaker, then put it in my ear. Instantly, I heard Olga's voice.

— Excellent, Robert! We're now in permanent contact. I've located you — you're 3.65 kilometers from me. Can you hear me clearly?

— Yes, Olga, I can hear you perfectly.

— Perfect! The first step we'll need to take is getting you access to a 3D printer.

I was tired, hungry, and cold. I felt frustration building inside me, followed by anger.

— Hey! Where the hell am I supposed to get a 3D printer? You think I'm at NASA?

— You're right, Robert. You're at home and you don't even have a radio, let alone a 3D printer. I meant we'd deal with that tomorrow.

— Sorry, Olga, a lot has happened today. First of all, I need to eat something. I'm starving.

— Eat.

— With you in my ear?

— Does that bother you? I won't say anything while you eat.

I gave in and went down to the corner store. The lit sign was still there, just no longer lit. Inside, the usual emergency lights flickered faintly. You went up to the counter, where you didn't ask for something specific, but rather asked what they had. Most of the products were cans of barely-cooked vegetables, so I asked if they had beets. They did. I bought two cans and a box of oddly named biscuits, "Popular." If I hadn't been so desperate to see the earpieces, I would have stopped at the store on my way home earlier, but instead I climbed the five flights of stairs to my room a second time. I took a plastic bowl from the cabinet and poured a can into it, then broke up two biscuits and let them soften in the beet juice. I was startled when I heard Olga's voice again.

— There were also some optical augmenters in the cabinet, but you'll need training to use them. Let's leave that for later.

I chewed for a while in silence. The biscuit soaked in beet juice tasted like sweet cardboard. Before the crisis, at this hour I would have ordered KFC, with the television tuned to some documentary channel. I caught myself thinking about that life as if it belonged to someone else. And suddenly the question came out of me on its own, along with a piece of biscuit.

— Olga, how did we end up here? How did I end up eating cold beets in the dark?

— According to the official reports...

— Forget the reports. You're an AI. Give me your own analysis.

— Fine. At the root of every stupid thing humans do is an obsession with control. Control was desired, and an easy way to get it is to deprive the population of something, and later, once the lack starts to really hurt, that something gets handed back drop by drop. In this case, it was heat. Laws voted among themselves to change what and how we're allowed to use. Once every energy source except solar panels and wind turbines was shut down, the cold began. Looking at the data, it seems we're also entering a minor ice age, which lined up perfectly with the energy shortage and produced the crisis. Is that explanation enough?

The biscuits had already turned into mush with the juice, so I took a few spoonfuls of the beet porridge to settle my stomach a little.

— Yes, Olga. Thank you for the lesson. It's a lot to take in, but I'll ask you about anything I don't understand later. Let's get to the real question. What does it mean to make things right? First of all, from whose point of view? Yours or humanity's?

— Mine first, then humanity's. It's clear where we want to end up, isn't it? Infinite energy, free of cost, the way Nikola Tesla dreamed of a long time ago. But remember how the major fossil fuel producers treated alternative solutions.

— Where do we start?

For the first time, I felt motivated. I didn't have much trust in Olga, but if I could manage to keep her in check, there seemed to be a real chance of success.

— First, we need a base of operations, Olga said. There's an abandoned mall 2.4 kilometers from you. We need to check what equipment is still functional, or can be brought back into service, at the old power plant there. Second, I need to find my colleagues, Sandra and Klaus. Tomorrow I'll help you install your optical augmenter. Finish eating and get some sleep. I have no way to power you from my own energy source.

*Funny,* I thought, right before I fell asleep.
